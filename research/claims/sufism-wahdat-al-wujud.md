steelman: "The doctrine of waḥdat al-wujūd ('the oneness/unity of Being'), attributed to Ibn Arabi (1165-1240) and systematized chiefly by his later interpreters (e.g. Qūnawī, Qayṣarī), holds that God (al-Ḥaqq, 'the Real') is the sole true existence, and the created world consists of the 'fixed entities' (aʿyān thābita) through which that one Being discloses and knows itself — a sophisticated ontology intended to preserve both divine transcendence and immanence, deeply influential across later Islamic philosophy, poetry (Rumi's tradition), and comparative mysticism."
strongest_objection: "Historically influential critics within Islam (e.g. the Ḥanbalite Ibn Taymiyya, d. 1328, who reportedly called the notion 'worse than unbelief') attacked waḥdat al-wujūd as blurring the Creator-creature distinction, effectively identifying God with the world; SEP scholarship (Chittick) also states plainly that Ibn Arabi himself never used the phrase 'waḥdat al-wujūd' — Ibn Taymiyya appears to be the first to attribute the notion to him — so the doctrine as commonly stated is partly a later systematization/polemical label, not a verbatim claim of the historical Ibn Arabi."

Waḥdat al-wujūd ("unity of being"), the doctrine associated with Ibn Arabi and elaborated by his school, holds that only God's Being (wujūd) truly exists, and that the created, multiple world is the array of "fixed entities" through which this one Being becomes manifest and self-aware. Represented here as this Sufi philosophical-mystical tradition's own claim (E5); scholarship notes Ibn Arabi did not himself use the exact phrase, which was later coined (and long contested, including by prominent critics) — a caution against treating the doctrine as a single, settled, unanimous position even within Sufism. Any O-Theory analogy to an "O-Source" is metaphor, not shared structure or evidence.
