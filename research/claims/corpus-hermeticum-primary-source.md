steelman: "The Corpus Hermeticum is a real, datable body of Greco-Egyptian philosophical-religious writing (17 tractates attributed pseudepigraphically to Hermes Trismegistus, a Hellenistic fusion of Hermes and Thoth), historically important for Renaissance Hermeticism after Marsilio Ficino's 1471 Latin translation, and a legitimate object of textual and historical scholarship (Copenhaver's critical edition is the modern scholarly standard). It contains genuine ancient articulations of mentalism, cosmic sympathy, and the human mind's kinship with the divine mind (nous)."
strongest_objection: "As philosophy/theology, its doctrines (the cosmos as a living, ensouled unity; the possibility of divinizing gnosis) are not empirically decidable and should be read as that tradition's own claims (E5), not as science. Note also that Isaac Casaubon demonstrated in 1614 (De rebus sacris et ecclesiasticis exercitationes XVI) that the Greek texts postdate Plato by their Koine Greek style and philosophical vocabulary, dating most of the corpus to c. 300 CE, refuting the Renaissance belief that Hermes Trismegistus was a contemporary of Moses — a real historical correction worth preserving alongside the corpus's genuine antiquity relative to modern popularizations like the Kybalion. (A partial qualification: the 17th-century scholar Ralph Cudworth later argued Casaubon's forgery charge cleanly applies to only 3 of the 17 treatises, so 'the whole corpus is a late forgery' overstates Casaubon's actual finding — the correct summary is 'later than claimed, not a single forged unit.') Symbol/practice, not fact."
falsifiability: "Symbolic/religious-philosophical text, not an empirical claim; not falsifiable as physics. The dating and transmission history, however, are ordinary historical/philological questions that have been settled to a first approximation (Casaubon 1614, refined by later scholarship including Cudworth's partial correction; modern papyrological and stylistic dating to c. 100-300 CE)."

Filed E5: a genuine ancient philosophical-religious corpus, represented as its tradition's own claims about mind, cosmos, and divinity, not as established fact.
