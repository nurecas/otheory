steelman: "If Mary, confined to a black-and-white room, could in principle acquire literally all physical information about color vision and neural processing, yet upon release and seeing a red tomato for the first time appears to learn something new (what red looks like), then there are truths about experience not entailed by any physical truths — a direct argument against physicalism as a complete metaphysics of mind, originally intended by Jackson as support for property dualism/epiphenomenalism."
strongest_objection: "The 'ability hypothesis' (Lewis, Nemirow) replies that Mary gains only new abilities or know-how (to recognize, imagine, and remember red) rather than new propositional knowledge, so no fact escapes the physicalist story — though SEP details substantial objections to this hypothesis as well (sec. 4.4). A-posteriori physicalists (the 'new knowledge/old fact' view, e.g. Loar) instead grant Mary learns a new fact but argue it is the very same physical/functional fact presented under a new phenomenal concept, so nothing metaphysically extra is shown. Critically, Jackson himself recanted the argument's anti-physicalist conclusion: SEP confirms that in Jackson (1995) he argued the very epiphenomenalism the thought experiment was meant to support is self-undermining (if qualia are epiphenomenal, we could not know about them), and by Jackson (2003, 2007) he had adopted 'strong representationalism' in defense of physicalism instead. The debate over which response succeeds remains unresolved and irreducibly philosophical."
falsifiability: "Not empirically decidable even in principle: the thought experiment is a conceptual test of whether propositional/factual knowledge is exhausted by physical description, not a claim about any measurable neural or behavioral outcome; the ability-hypothesis/new-fact dispute cannot be settled by any experiment since both sides agree on all the physical and behavioral data. Argued by reason; E4."

The knowledge argument is one of the two canonical arguments (with the zombie/conceivability argument) used to motivate the explanatory gap that underwrites panpsychism, dual-aspect monism, and idealism alike — it is cited by name in Kastrup's and Goff's cases against physicalism. It must be represented with its serious rebuttals (ability hypothesis, a posteriori physicalism, and Jackson's own recantation) intact, since it is frequently deployed in popular accounts as a "knockdown" argument against physicalism when the professional literature treats it as very much contested. E4 — an argument, never evidence.
