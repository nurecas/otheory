steelman: "Brewer et al. (2011) scanned experienced meditators (concentration, loving-kindness, and choiceless-awareness practices) against meditation-naive controls and found the DMN's main hub regions (medial prefrontal and posterior cingulate cortices) were relatively deactivated in experienced meditators across all three meditation types, both during meditation and, notably, during ordinary rest/cognitive tasks — with stronger anticorrelation between the dorsal-attention and default-mode networks in meditators, consistent with less mind-wandering and self-referential processing. This links a specific, trainable contemplative practice to a measurable, dose-dependent (with practice hours) brain-network signature, a rare case of a first-person practice tradition converging with a quantifiable neural marker."
strongest_objection: "The core design in both cited studies is cross-sectional (comparing experienced meditators to novices) rather than a randomized longitudinal trial, so self-selection is a live and largely unaddressed confound in these specific papers: people with naturally lower DMN self-referential activity, or particular personality/trait differences, may be more likely to take up and sustain meditation practice in the first place, rather than meditation causing the difference. Sample sizes are modest (Brewer et al.'s meditator group was small and highly experienced, a further selection filter on top of the practice itself) and 'DMN activity reduction' is a coarse proxy for the much richer first-person reports of jhāna/samādhi states; the neuroscience has not mapped onto specific traditionally-described stages (see jhana-samadhi-absorption-states) with any precision. Framing this finding as if longitudinal randomized designs 'generally support a training effect' should be read cautiously: the two sources actually cited here are both cross-sectional, so any causal claim goes beyond what these two papers themselves demonstrate."
falsifiability: "Empirical/neuroimaging claim: falsifiable via longitudinal randomized designs (meditation-naive subjects randomized to training vs. control, scanned pre/post) to separate causation from self-selection. The two sources cited here (Brewer 2011, Garrison 2015) are both cross-sectional comparisons of experienced meditators vs. novices, not randomized trials; the claim is tiered E3 as a real, replicated correlational neuroscience finding, with the causal (training-effect) interpretation held as more tentative than the correlational one and not fully established by these specific sources."

This is an EMPIRICAL/NEUROSCIENCE claim (E3): long-term meditation practice is associated with reduced default-mode-network self-referential activity in cross-sectional comparisons of experienced meditators vs. novices. It is a brain-correlate finding about a trainable practice, kept separate from the first-person contemplative reports of jhāna/samādhi (E5, a much richer and stage-specific phenomenology not yet mapped onto this coarse neural measure) and from any metaphysical claim that quieting self-referential brain activity constitutes contact with a non-dual ground (E4). Supports O Theory's "valve" metaphor only as a candidate mechanism (reduced self-referential filtering), not as evidence for what lies beyond the filter.

**Red-team notes (2026-07-07):** Both sources verified — Brewer et al. 2011, PNAS 108(50):20254-20259, confirmed (experienced meditators across concentration/loving-kindness/choiceless-awareness practices vs. meditation-naive controls; DMN hub deactivation in mPFC/PCC across all three practice types, both during meditation and at rest; stronger dorsal-attention/DMN anticorrelation in meditators) — matches the claim's description precisely. Garrison et al. 2015 (published in journal 2015, PMC record shows a 2016 PMC posting date — both years appear across different indexing sources; treated as the same paper, no discrepancy of substance) confirmed via independent search: simultaneous EEG-fMRI, PCC connectivity reductions in meditators at rest and during meditation. Tier E3 correctly assigned, and downgraded confidence in the causal framing specifically: the draft's own falsifiability section claimed "several such [longitudinal randomized] studies exist and generally support a training effect," but neither cited source is a longitudinal randomized trial — both are cross-sectional. This is not a fabrication (longitudinal meditation-training RCTs showing DMN change do exist in the broader literature) but the claim file oversold what its own two cited sources establish; I have corrected the objection and falsifiability text to make clear the self-selection confound is live and unaddressed by Brewer/Garrison themselves, and that the "training effect" claim would need additional sources not currently cited to be fully supported. This is the most significant methodological correction made across this claim set.
