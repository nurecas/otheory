steelman: "The meta-problem reframes the debate usefully for all sides: explaining why humans across cultures report an explanatory gap, judge experience to be ineffable, and find zombies conceivable is itself a tractable, functional ('easy') problem for cognitive science, and Chalmers argues that a good solution to it could either debunk our belief in a hard problem (supporting illusionism) or instead explain why the appearance of a hard problem is exactly what we would expect if phenomenal consciousness is real and irreducible (supporting realism about the hard problem) — making the meta-problem a genuine, productive fork for adjudicating between camps without begging the question either way. The paper generated a full JCS symposium of replies (2019-2020) with a further response from Chalmers, indicating sustained engagement across the field."
strongest_objection: "Realists about consciousness (including Chalmers himself, who remains agnostic-to-realist) worry the meta-problem could be used illegitimately to 'debunk' consciousness reports the way one debunks reports of a rope mistaken for a snake — but unlike the rope case, the reporting phenomenon here is itself allegedly conscious, so a full functional explanation of the reports may leave the reality question exactly where it started, i.e., the meta-problem may not actually settle anything about the hard problem despite promising to, and critics dispute whether 'explaining the judgment' and 'explaining the experience' can be cleanly separated at all."
falsifiability: "The narrow empirical component (why people report and judge as they do) is cognitive science and could in principle be studied empirically; but the further metaphysical question of whether solving it dissolves, debunks, or leaves untouched the hard problem is not empirically decidable — that inferential step is argued by reason. Classified E4 because the claim of central interest here is the interpretive/metaphysical one, not the underlying cognitive-science observation."

The meta-problem is Chalmers's own reflexive move to keep the hard-problem debate honest: it separates the observation (people report and judge that consciousness is puzzling — an empirically tractable fact about human cognition) from its interpretation (whether this vindicates illusionism or realism about phenomenal consciousness — an E4 argument). This separation is a template O Theory should follow generally: report the cognitive-science observation at its actual tier and keep the metaphysical reading (e.g. "consciousness is fundamental") as a distinct, lower-certainty E4 claim layered on top, never fused into one statement.
