steelman: "First theorem: any consistent formal system F powerful enough to express elementary arithmetic contains statements that can neither be proved nor disproved within F. Second theorem: such a system cannot prove its own consistency (assuming it is consistent). These are precise, fully proved results in mathematical logic about formal derivability within specified axiomatic systems — a genuine theorem, correctly using that word."
strongest_objection: "The theorems are routinely over-extended outside formal logic: to claims that human minds provably exceed computers (Lucas/Penrose — SEP §6.3 confirms this requires the implausible premise that a mind can always verify its own consistency; the objection traces to Putnam 1960 and the anti-mechanist rebuttals are generally judged to fail), to theological arguments for the existence of unprovable-but-true cosmic truths, and to postmodern claims that 'all systems of thought are equally incomplete/relative.' Franzén (2005) and SEP both catalog these as misunderstandings: the theorems concern derivability in a specific formal system, not truth in any informal or absolute sense, and do not license metaphysical or mystical conclusions."
falsifiability: "Proven mathematics (Gödel 1931), not empirically falsifiable. What IS contestable, and where the theorems are frequently misapplied, is any claim that extends them by analogy to minds, physical reality, or spiritual truth without a stated formal system — those extensions are not proofs and should be tiered separately (typically E4 speculation or E6 if presented as established)."

Gödel's theorems are genuine, fully proved mathematics (E1) about the limits of provability inside sufficiently expressive formal axiomatic systems. They are flagged here explicitly because they are among the most mystically and philosophically over-invoked results in mathematics — used to argue everything from "science can never be complete" to "the universe requires a mind outside itself" to "human consciousness transcends computation." The Stanford Encyclopedia and Franzén's book-length treatment both catalog these as misapplications: the actual theorem is narrow and formal, and any leap to metaphysics is a separate, lower-tier claim (E4 at best) that must not borrow the theorem's E1 certainty.
