steelman: "Chalmers argues the zombie scenario — a being physically and functionally identical to a conscious human in every respect but with no phenomenal experience ('all is dark inside') — is conceivable without contradiction, and defends (via two-dimensional semantics) that idealized, coherent conceivability of this kind tracks metaphysical possibility; if zombies are possible, facts about consciousness are not necessitated by physical facts, so physicalism (which requires exactly that necessitation) is false. Chalmers treats the intuitive ease of conceiving zombies as 'the principal manifestation of the explanatory gap.'"
strongest_objection: "Type-B physicalists grant zombies are conceivable but deny conceivability entails possibility here, treating the physical-to-phenomenal link as a metaphysically necessary but only a-posteriori-knowable identity (like water=H2O, which is also 'conceivably' otherwise until you know the chemistry) — denying the key inference step rather than the premise; SEP frames this via the primary/secondary conceivability-possibility distinction from two-dimensional semantics. Others (Dennett) reject the conceivability premise itself, arguing zombies only seem conceivable because we have not fully specified what 'physically identical in every respect, including all functional/behavioral facts' actually requires, and that 'the putative contrast between zombies and conscious beings is illusory' once fully specified — if fully specified, the scenario is argued to be incoherent, not merely surprising."
falsifiability: "Not empirically decidable even in principle: no observation can show whether a given being that behaves identically to a conscious human has phenomenal experience or not (this is the classic other-minds problem sharpened to a metaphysical limit case), and the conceivability-to-possibility inference is a question of modal epistemology, not empirical fact. Argued by reason; E4."

Alongside the knowledge argument, this is Chalmers's other principal weapon against physicalism and the main formal engine behind treating consciousness as ontologically fundamental rather than reducible — directly relevant to whether O Theory's "consciousness is fundamental" premise has a rigorous philosophical pedigree (it does, as an argument) versus empirical support (it does not, and cannot in principle). The type-B physicalist and Dennettian objections are live and unresolved in the literature; this claim must not be presented as settling the case for non-reductionism. E4 throughout.
