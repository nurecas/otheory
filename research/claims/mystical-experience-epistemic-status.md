steelman: "Proponents of an evidential reading (Swinburne's 'principle of credulity': things are as they perceptually seem unless we have specific reason to doubt) argue mystical experience has genuine phenomenological content, is embedded in practices allowing some procedural cross-checking within a tradition, shows cross-tradition convergence on certain features (per Stace), and produces behavioral fruits — enough, on a permissive epistemology, to count as at least weak prima facie evidence for its apparent object. Alston's doxastic-practice defense adds that sense-perception itself rests on unavoidable epistemic circularity, so demanding a higher standard of independent verification for mystical experience than we demand of perception is arguably a double standard."
strongest_objection: "The dominant objection (Fales and others) is the lack of cross-checkability: unlike ordinary perception, there is no independent, tradition-neutral procedure to check a mystical report against an external, publicly accessible state of affairs, and no instrument analogous to a telescope or thermometer. The religious-diversity problem compounds this: mystics across traditions report incompatible metaphysical contents (a personal theistic God vs. an impersonal non-dual Brahman vs. Buddhist non-self/emptiness), and if the experience straightforwardly tracked reality, this divergence at the level of content — not just interpretation — is hard to explain away without conceding that at least some traditions' 'perceptions' are non-veridical, which undercuts the inference to any of them being reliably veridical. SEP additionally notes William James's own observation that the supposed 'eternal unanimity' among mystics substantially disappears under closer scrutiny — the convergence claim that motivates the evidential reading is itself contested at the descriptive level, before any epistemological argument is even applied. There is no consensus resolution in the philosophical literature; the field's honest state is a standoff between competing, internally coherent epistemic frameworks (Jamesian/Swinburnian credulism vs. Fales-style cross-checkability skepticism), not a near-resolution leaning either way."
falsifiability: "Not empirically decidable even in principle; this is the E4 epistemology-of-religious-experience debate itself (perceptual model vs. doxastic-practice defenses vs. cross-checkability and diversity objections), with no consensus. It is the claim that governs how strongly any of the other claims in this domain (Stace's typology, Forman's PCE, the psilocybin/MEQ findings, DMN correlates) may be read as bearing on metaphysical truth — and the honest current answer, per mainstream philosophy of religion, is that they are not established as evidence for their apparent content. This is the sharpest illustration in the domain of the project's core distinction: a real, measurable experience (E2/E3) does not make its metaphysical content (E4) true, and no falsification criterion exists for the metaphysical content itself — only for the philosophical arguments about whether the experience counts as evidence."
